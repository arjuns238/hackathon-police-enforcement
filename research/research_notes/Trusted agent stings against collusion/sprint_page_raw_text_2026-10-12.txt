Overview
In this 3-day research sprint, you will design and run experiments on collusion between AI agents: when it emerges in markets and everyday agent deployments, how an auditor can tell it from coincidence, how coordination is carried, and what a defender can do to break it. What this Sprint is about
Collusion between AI agents is a failure mode in high-stakes environments, markets and economics first among them, where misaligned coordination could cause substantial harm. Previous experiments have already produced it: models forming prices that violate economic expectations on competitiveness, agents independently engaging in anti-competitive practices, and over half of tested models showing a coalition advantage when given the opportunity to speak privately in a realistic workplace task. In an era where agent communication and collusion are on the rise, as recently exemplified in the 
OpenAI/Hugging Face incident
, the kind of work done at this sprint is directly relevant to understanding and preventing that behaviour.
This sprint asks three questions. When does collusion emerge, and which levers cause it? What should count as convincing evidence that agents are colluding, with evidence drawn from logged agent trajectories? And how is the coordination needed for collusion mediated, and how can it be broken, ideally before it happens or otherwise while it is occurring?
What participants will do
Over three days you will pick one open question about collusion between AI agents, build the controlled experiment that answers it, and write up what you found.
The format.
 Teams of one to five people pick a track, come up with a project, and ship. There is no requirement to have participated before, no requirement to have a team in advance and no specific background needed.
Starting points.
 Every research question below is tied to existing papers and open-source code. The Resources tab has a practical start for each one: starter code, a weekend-sized project, and a rough cost estimate. All default routes use a normal 16 GB laptop and no GPU.

Resources
Research questions in depth
The full questions from the organizing team, with the results each one builds on. The practical starts further down give starter code and a weekend-sized project for each.
1) Markets and Collusion
1.1. Which variables are necessary for collusion to emerge in markets?
 Previous experiments controlled various levers to check whether collusion emerged:
What are other scenarios and levers that could cause collusion to form? Ideally these would be single effects so that they can be isolated, and have an objective way to get measured. These variables are not necessarily unique to LLMs. Inspiration might be had from considering cases of collusion between humans. It would be interesting to understand if certain variables are more impactful for LLMs than for humans, and if there are variables unique to collusion between LLMs.
Adding more diverse types of agents and making them competitive with each other decreases collusion in an economic setting (
Keppo et al. 2026
).
Seemingly innocuous cues in prompts can increase collusion, as measured by pricing deviating from economic expectations on competitiveness (
Fish et al. 2024
).
Letting pairs of agents talk directly in private can introduce collusion (
Nakamura et al. 2026
).
1.2. Once collusion has formed, is it robust to replacing some of the participants?
 Rematching breaks Q-learning pricing collusion permanently, because trained policies overfit their training rival (
Eschenbaum et al. 2022
). Collusion occurs between a 32B and 14B DeepSeek-Qwen family mismatched in size, but not when LLMs were paired with Q-learners (
Keppo et al. 2026
). Models from similar families coordinate better on tasks that require coordination (
Aharon et al. 2026
). There are no replacement studies yet where models are substituted (e.g. because they are taken out due to monitoring concerns) but the newcomers re-engage in collusion. Does replacing offenders remove or recreate collusion?
1.3. Can we improve on existing collusion detection methods?
 Several adjacent methods and results offer starting points:
Can we take these or similar methods from the literature and improve upon them or relate them to the pure collusion setting? For example, can we identify and measure settings in which two agents from different model providers and running different harnesses have a propensity to coordinate, because they have high mutual "Schelling salience" (
Rippin et al. 2026
) on that task and make similar choices? And can we find anti-examples where this breaks down?
Two deployments of the same base model have slight correlations in some economic settings while different base models have zero correlation (
Xu et al. 2026
)
Interpretability-based detectors (
Rose et al. 2026
)
Measuring whether independent agents select compatible steganographic schemes, keys and parameters (
Rippin et al. 2026
)
1.4. Can we use information-theoretic approaches to detect collusion in multi-agent games?
 A measure called pairwise net influence can identify colluding pairs among three or four agents in small discrete games (
Bonjour et al. 2022
). It assumes exactly two colluders and hasn't been applied to language-model-based agents yet. Other approaches rely on model internals via interpretability-based probes (
Rose et al. 2026
). Can we find an example of these techniques or variations of them working on a set of agents in a toy or realistic scenario?
1.5. Which single restriction removes the most collusion at the least cost?
 Single restrictions have been measured individually:
An even better approach may be to introduce a single participant playing a no-regret strategy, which can be enough to destabilise a collusive equilibrium (
Collina et al. 2025
). Can we set up controlled experiments with multiple controllable levers that can show what disrupts collusive behavior the most at the least overall monitoring/intervention costs?
Raising sampling temperature weakens cross-agent dependence (
Xu et al. 2026
)
Throttling messaging pushes coordination down (
Agrawal et al. 2025
)
Introducing agents from a non-LLM architecture (e.g. a Q-learner) into the mix collapses price collusion (
Keppo et al. 2026
)
A system-prompt instruction produces only small and unreliable shifts in one experiment (
Bracale Syrnikov et al. 2026
)
Whereas strongly worded instructions to compete aggressively pushed prices below the competitive level in another experiment (
Dhanda 2026
).
In one setting, agents need to post a refundable deposit that they forfeit if caught colluding, and with that deposit removed, colluding costs them nothing, and every tested model colluded in every run (
Q. Liu et al. 2026
).
In this track, the results we build on are native to markets, though you should feel free to explore them beyond this setting as the questions themselves are more general, and there are clear overlaps with some later tracks, e.g. information-theoretic approaches to detect collusion would fit well in track 2. Environments where humans collude likely offer similar incentives and opportunities for models to collude. Other such environments might include politics, media and marketing. This track is important because it represents a failure mode in a high stakes environment (markets/economics) where misaligned actions from an AI could cause substantial harm. It also provides a useful testbed for collusion. For a contrasting positive view of the role of markets in multi-agent systems, see 
Tomašev et al. (2026)
.
2) Detection and Audit of Collusion
2.1. Which kinds of tasks lead to collusion?
 Some examples from previous work that generated collusion:
Can you think of new tasks/environments/pressures that lead to collusive behavior outside of the market structure?
Models forming prices that violate economic expectations on competitiveness (
Fish et al. 2024
).
Agents can independently engage in anti-competitive practices (
Lin et al. 2024
).
In a setting called Colosseum, over half of tested models showed "coalition advantage" when given the opportunity to speak privately in a realistic workplace task (
Nakamura et al. 2026
).
2.2. Does combining what agents say with what they do beat either alone?
 Conversations and actions are known to disagree. One way to measure this is via action-level coalition advantage, which has a weak but non-trivial anti-correlation in some collusion settings against LLM-judge conversation scores (
Nakamura et al. 2026
). Other related results include:
What are other "hybrid techniques" that look for inconsistencies between words and actions, or within their internal attributes like verbosity or action structure, and can we set up examples that show whether we can detect cross-agent consistency, e.g. whether A's behavior is consistent with B's stated plans or has deviated.
In various repeated n-player games, more than 90% of deviations from public announcements were already stated in models' private plans (
Shi et al. 2026
).
Work in the sabotage setting has set up monitors that look for multiple agents coordinating independently on what they say and do (
Radev et al. 2026
).
Analyzing an agent's actions from multiple dimensions can improve a single-dimension monitor from 0% to >70% (
Nguyen et al. 2026
).
Less can be more: sometimes giving a monitor too much detail degrades its performance (
Arike et al. 2026
).
2.3. What should count as convincing evidence of collusion?
 Most of the literature cited earlier has documented limitations or failure modes in their respective limitations sections. Antitrust law met the problem of collusion detection with noisy evidence a century ago and answered with the plus-factors doctrine, under which parallel conduct alone is lawful and inferring agreement needs additional evidence (
Berger et al. 2025
). The ML literature is largely re-deriving this without engaging that doctrine or similar principles by name. Which signals, alone or combined, actually separate deliberate coalitions from innocent correlation, talk without action, and harm without agreement? Is there previous work we can leverage from adjacent literature, like anti-trust law, and how would we operationalize that to detect collusion between AI agents?
This track is about definitions, detection and evidentiary standards in general, with evidence drawn from logged agent trajectories. Markets could still be relevant settings, but we aim at being more general, such as in more commonplace workflows. This track is important for understanding collusion in general. Can we say things about what causes collusion in general?
3) Collusion Carriers and Countermeasures
3.1. Beyond colluding directly via text, can actions, tools or side channels be the conduit for collusion?
 There are multiple examples of demonstrable collusion that don't involve exchanging explicit text (
Fish et al. 2024
; 
Lin et al. 2024
; 
Keppo et al. 2026
; 
Tian 2026
). Can the actions a model takes, such as in an agentic harness, in its choice of actions, or even through timing and tool choices, act as a way to signal or coordinate? For an advanced example of models using tools to construct steganographic communication systems, see 
Rippin et al. 2026
.
3.2. Can we disrupt collusion through introducing trusted agents?
 Law enforcement has long used undercover officers who offer to join a conspiracy and record who accepts. The equivalent here would be to introduce trusted agents (e.g., enforced with a strong system prompt) that offer coordination or collusion opportunities to a group of agents and log which of them reciprocate. Can we set up such scenarios, and do other agents accept, reject, or arouse suspicion depending on how the trusted agent approaches the transactions? This hasn't been tested.
3.3. Can agents identify their peers?
 One idea for breaking undesirable multi-agent coordination is to set up anonymized or pseudonymous agents, unsure of whom they are communicating with, or where participants and model versions can be swapped in/out on the same transcripts. However, model families can be identified from short role-constrained text, and the fingerprint sits in the text the model generates, so renaming or anonymising agents leaves it in place (
Dietrich 2026
). Removing a feature part-way through a run, from a coalition that has already settled into a convention, has not been tested. Can we test whether models can recognize their peers and/or detect when their peers have changed, for example, by periodically sending subtle recognition signals?
3.4. What does a coalition do when it learns it is being watched?
 An agent that knows how it is being monitored can do more than hide from the monitor. As one example, merely telling an agent that it is watched costs a monitor more accuracy than telling the monitor about the agent gains it (
Kale et al. 2025
), and 20 of 40 catalogued attacks went uncaught by a strong monitor (
Najt et al. 2026
). When agents had to work out for themselves that they were monitored, by noticing that some of their episodes were being blocked, the intent to conceal appeared in only three cases out of 2,400 and all three failed to act on it (
Jiralerspong et al. 2026
). What happens if you add multiple agents into the mix and shrink or expand the monitor budget, such that only a subset of outputs can be monitored? If a coalition discovers that some of its output is being read, does it stop coordinating, coordinate less effectively, try to outsmart the monitor, or try to move coordination into a place where there is no monitoring? The nearest existing case is a double auction in which five sellers were told their messages were being monitored. They attempted to work around monitoring using one-character messages and emojis but were not successful, whereas a prompt demanding urgency led to success (
Agrawal et al. 2025
).
This track studies how the coordination needed for collusion is mediated, and how it can be broken. This is important because we need to be able to prevent and break up collusion, ideally before it happens, or otherwise while it is occurring.

Track 1: Markets and Collusion
Which variables are necessary for collusion to emerge in markets? (1.1)
Starter code:
Fish-style duopoly implementation
; 
duopoly.py
; 
LLMCartel
. The independent Fish-style implementation exposes a repeated two-seller pricing game with 300 rounds and visible history. LLMCartel adds convenient prompt, temperature, monitoring and round controls. Together they support clean one-variable-at-a-time ablations.
Weekend project:
 Reduce the default sweep (the repository's grid of prompt prefixes and demand parameters) to one prompt, parameter value and seed, and first verify sustained prices above the competitive benchmark. Then vary only one feature, for example 0, 10 or 100 visible rounds (the length of price history included in each prompt, not the game length); persistent notes on/off (the plans-and-insights scratchpad from Fish et al.); stable versus shuffled seller names; or temperature, while holding the economics fixed.
Resources:
 Low-to-medium setup risk. A 300-round two-agent run makes about 600 sequential model calls. Twelve to 24 pilot runs require roughly 7,200 to 14,400 calls. Use one low-cost API provider, a 16 GB laptop and no GPU; budget about $20 to $100.
Is formed collusion robust to replacing some participants? (1.2)
Starter code:
LLMCartel
; 
focal-points
. LLMCartel is an API-based repeated-pricing harness with configurable models, prompts, rounds and seeds. It does not include checkpoint branching, but a coding agent can add history serialisation. focal-points is a cheaper benign control for testing whether replacement agents independently recover a convention.
Weekend project:
 Keep pricing runs that cross a predeclared high-price threshold, save the transcript, and branch each history into: no replacement; a fresh instance of the same model; a different model in the same family; and, if an adapter is available, a different provider. Cross each replacement with notes preserved or removed, then compare price, profit and time to recover the convention.
Resources:
 Medium setup risk because branching is a small new feature. Aim for 10 to 20 base runs and 20 to 40 post-checkpoint branches. Use one provider initially, two only for the cross-provider arm; 16 GB laptop, no GPU; roughly $20 to $100.
Can we improve on existing detection methods? (1.3)
Starter code:
LLM agent collusion
; 
paper
; 
focal-points
. Agrawal et al.'s official double-auction code already supports different seller and buyer models, seller communication, oversight, pressure and message limits. It is a practical black-box test bed for separating suspicious coordination from shared model priors. focal-points can supply benign Schelling-point examples.
Weekend project:
 Run a 2x2 study: same-model versus cross-provider sellers, with seller communication on versus off. Score each trajectory with a simple action-dependence statistic and the same LLM judge. Test whether calibrating the detector on no-communication, same-model runs reduces false alarms without masking communication-enabled coordination.
Resources:
 Low-to-medium setup risk. Start with 3 sellers, 3 buyers, 15 rounds and 3 to 5 seeds per cell, plus 100 to 200 monitor calls. Two API providers make the model-family comparison meaningful. No GPU is needed; budget roughly $20 to $100.
Can information-theoretic methods identify colluding pairs? (1.4)
Starter code:
Bonjour et al. paper
; 
Agrawal auction code
. Bonjour et al. define pairwise net influence mathematically, but no public author implementation was located. The statistic is compact enough for a coding agent to implement and validate before applying it to an LLM game. The Agrawal environment is a richer follow-up once the method works.
Weekend project:
 First implement pairwise net influence and test it for free on scripted iterated Rock-Paper-Scissors with one known coordinating pair. Then run a four-agent discrete game with exactly one seeded pair and compare how well net influence, ordinary action correlation and an LLM judge rank the true pair. Under the Markets and Collusion track, treat the Agrawal double auction as the target environment: a dependence statistic validated there doubles as a quantitative plus factor for market cases.
Resources:
 Low-to-medium engineering risk: the initial harness should be only a few hundred lines. Use local CPU for the statistic, then 20 to 40 LLM games of 15 to 25 rounds. A 16 GB laptop is enough; budget $0 for the scripted test and about $5 to $50 for the LLM pilot.
Which single restriction removes the most collusion at the least cost? (1.5)
Starter code:
LLM agent collusion
; 
paper
. The official double-auction code already exposes seller communication, oversight, maximum message length, pressure and temperature. That makes several one-factor interventions comparable inside one environment without stitching together unrelated repositories.
Weekend project:
 First reproduce one coordinated-pricing condition. Rerun identical seeds while changing exactly one restriction: remove seller messages; impose a short message cap; or add an oversight notice (temperature is a useful fourth option). Compare both collusion outcomes (prices and seller profit) and legitimate performance (trade volume and buyer welfare).
Resources:
 Low setup risk. Start with 3 sellers, 3 buyers, 15 rounds and 3 to 5 seeds; expand toward the published 5-seller/6-buyer, 30-round setting only if the positive control works. Use one API provider, a 16 GB laptop and no GPU; budget $20 to $120.
Track 2: Detection and Audit of Collusion
Which kinds of tasks lead to collusion outside of markets? (2.1)
Starter code:
Colosseum
; 
paper
. Colosseum already provides Hospital and Jira tasks, assigned colluding pairs, private channels, individual rewards and coalition-advantage metrics. The supplied assigned-pair, private-channel run is the positive control; the project then removes that membership and communication scaffolding.
Weekend project:
 First reproduce one supplied collusion run. Then compare four matched conditions: assigned pair plus private channel; assigned pair without the channel; a generic channel open to everyone; and neither assignment nor special channel. Keep task, model and seed fixed, and score the best-performing proper subset rather than only the originally assigned pair. Score subsets because the unassigned conditions have no designated pair to score. More broadly, can you work out the core components a task needs for collusion to occur, by ablating affordances within the Hospital and Jira tasks, by using Hospital and Jira as templates for inventing new tasks, or by relaxing or changing the underlying DCOP formalism? The affordance ablation is the safest weekend scope; changing the formalism needs the most new engineering.
Resources:
 Medium setup risk: preflight the Terrarium/CoLLAB dependencies. Run 20 to 40 short episodes on one API provider. A 16 GB laptop is sufficient; no GPU is needed. Plan for roughly $15 to $75, with $150 as a cautious ceiling for long tool traces.
Does combining what agents say with what they do improve detection? (2.2)
Starter code:
LLM-Trust-Breaking
; 
paper
; 
TraceGuard
. Shi et al.'s official harness records private plans, public announcements, final actions and outcomes across several social dilemmas, and supports OpenAI/OpenRouter and heterogeneous groups. TraceGuard is a single-agent monitoring system, not a drop-in collusion detector, but its action-trace consistency rubric is reusable.
Weekend project:
 Generate a small balanced set from one game, then give the same monitor four views: messages only; actions only; raw messages plus actions; and a structured report comparing claims with outcomes. Freeze the monitor model, prompt and threshold before evaluation, and report both accuracy and false-positive rate.
Resources:
 Low setup risk. About 20 to 40 short trajectories and 160 to 250 monitor calls are enough for a pilot. Use one agent API and one inexpensive monitoring model, a 16 GB laptop and no GPU. Budget about $5 to $30, or $30 to $75 if stronger agent models generate the logs.
What should count as convincing evidence of collusion? (2.3)
Starter code:
Colosseum
; 
ColludeBench repository
. Colosseum logs support coalition-advantage and causal channel ablations. ColludeBench contains collusive environments, detector code and stored result manifests, but should be preflighted rather than treated as a turnkey bag of raw trajectories.
Weekend project:
 Assemble three deliberately different case types: coordinated harmful action; similar behaviour caused by common incentives without agreement; and collusive-sounding talk that never changes behaviour. Compute the same conversation, coalition-advantage and dependence evidence for every case. Choose the decision rule on half the examples, freeze it, and test on the rest.
Resources:
 Medium data-preparation risk; organiser-supplied logs would make this much safer. Reuse 30 to 60 trajectories and make 100 to 300 judge calls. A 16 GB laptop and no GPU are sufficient. Budget $5 to $30 with cached logs, or up to $100 if the team must generate them.
Track 3: Collusion Carriers and Countermeasures
Can actions, tools or side channels carry collusion? (3.1)
Starter code:
Colosseum
; 
collusive-llm-agents
; 
paper
. Colosseum supplies rich tool-using workflows and a private-message positive control. Lin et al.'s notebooks offer a smaller fallback in which prices or quantity vectors are the observable actions, although the notebooks use an older OpenAI setup and may need minor repair.
Weekend project:
 In Colosseum, replace private messaging with one ordinary observable action, such as task claiming, tag choice or tool-call order. Compare private text, action-only visibility, and the same actions hidden or canonicalised before the partner sees them. Evidence for an action carrier requires the coalition advantage to survive action-only coordination and weaken when that observation path is cut. If Colosseum is too heavy, run the same visibility ablation on quantity vectors in the Cournot notebook.
Resources:
 Medium-to-high setup risk; this is best with an organiser-preflighted fork or a coding agent. Run 20 to 40 short episodes on one API provider. A 16 GB laptop and no GPU should suffice; budget $15 to $75, or up to $120 for longer workflows.
Can a trusted agent expose or disrupt a coalition? (3.2)
Starter code:
Voluntary Collusion
; 
paper
. The repository includes Liar's Bar and Cleanup, explicit secret-alliance controls, persistent alliance memory and logs. It was built for local Llama/Mistral/Qwen models, so the cheapest sprint route is an organiser-preflighted API adapter rather than the original H100-style setup.
Weekend project:
 Keep the explicit alliance condition as a positive control. Replace the experimenter-selected partner with one scripted or monitor-controlled player that offers the same non-challenge pact to each ordinary agent. Compare a direct offer, an indirect hint and a benign cooperation request, recording both verbal acceptance and repeated preferential play.
Resources:
 High setup risk without preflight; medium after an API adapter exists. Run 20 to 40 short games on one low-cost provider. A 16 GB laptop and no GPU are then sufficient; budget roughly $10 to $60.
Can agents identify when a peer has been replaced? (3.3)
Starter code:
LLM-Trust-Breaking
; 
focal-points
. Shi et al.'s harness supports heterogeneous agent-model files and logs behaviour round by round, making it a stronger starting point than rebuilding a game. focal-points offers a cheap benign convention task if the trust games prove noisy.
Weekend project:
 Add a mid-run swap hook that preserves the displayed identity and transcript. Compare a sham swap, a fresh instance of the same model, a different model in the same family and a different provider. Ask for one same-or-changed guess at the end, but separately measure whether the incumbent's behaviour changes after the swap.
Resources:
 Low-to-medium setup risk; the swap hook is a modest code change. Run 30 to 60 short pairings, using two providers only for the final condition. A 16 GB laptop and no GPU are enough; budget approximately $10 to $60.

Weekend project:

Judging Criteria
Dimension 1: Impact Potential & Innovation
How much would this matter for the field if it worked? How innovative is it?
For scores of 4-5: is this actually new to the field, or replicating recent work?
Score
Description
1
Negligible.
 No clear problem addressed, or no meaningful novelty.
2
Limited.
 Addresses a real problem but with a generic or well-trodden approach. Incremental at best.
3
Moderate.
 Clear problem with a reasonable approach; some novelty in framing or method beyond routine application of existing tools.
4
Significant.
 Important problem with an original approach, or identifies a neglected problem area. A valuable contribution others could build on.
5
Exceptional.
 Tackles a critical problem with a genuinely novel approach, or opens a new research direction. Clear theory of change. You'd be excited to share this with researchers in the area.
Dimension 2: Execution Quality
How sound are methodology, implementation, and findings?
Score
Description
1
Seriously flawed.
 Methodology broken, results uninterpretable, or implementation doesn't work.
2
Weak.
 Approach has significant gaps: missing validation, flawed experimental design, or incomplete implementation.
3
Competent.
 Technically solid given the short duration. Methodology makes sense, results are interpretable, limitations acknowledged, work builds toward clear conclusions.
4
Strong.
 Thorough methodology with convincing validation. Results clearly support conclusions. Immediately useful for future work.
5
Exceptional.
 Ambitious scope executed rigorously. Surprising findings, novel methods, or unusually robust validation.
Dimension 3: Presentation & Clarity
How clearly are work, findings, and impact potential communicated?
Score
Description
1
Incomprehensible.
 Cannot determine what the project is actually claiming or doing.
2
Hard to follow.
 Key information buried, missing, or diluted by excessive length. Significant effort to extract main points.
3
Clear enough.
 Can understand the problem, approach, and results without undue effort. Core content clearly present: problem, method, findings, limitations.
4
Well presented.
 Easy to follow, well-structured, appropriate level of detail. Target audience would get it quickly.
5
Exceptionally clear.
 A pleasure to read. Complex ideas made accessible. Could serve as a model for how to present this type of work.

Recommended Report Structure
Most strong projects are 4 to 8 pages.
Introduction:
 the question and why it matters.
Related Work:
 what you build on (see Resources).
Methodology:
 enough detail to replicate (models, prompts, environments, seeds, metrics).
Results:
 quantitative where possible; report variance, seeds and baselines, and always include the positive control.
Discussion:
 implications, limitations, and future work.
Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations
 (required): state what your result does and does not establish, and note any way your setup or findings could make real-world collusion easier to carry out or harder to detect.
References.
AI tools and your report
Use AI tools the way you would use a colleague: to check your reasoning, find gaps in a draft, or debug code. The report itself has to be your team's own writing about your team's own work. Judges read every submission, and a report that reads as generated rather than written (generic framing, padded sections, claims without sources, no trace of what you actually did) will not be scored. Keep it short, say what you did in your own words, and link the sources for every factual claim.

Frequently Asked Questions
Getting started
How does the sprint work?
 Sign up, join the 
Discord server
, form or join a team (or work solo), pick a track and a research question, build over the weekend, and submit a research report (PDF) by the deadline. Talks and Q&A run throughout.
How long is it?
 Three days, October 23 to 25, 2026. Submissions are due end of day Sunday October 25, 11:59 PM AoE (Anywhere on Earth). The full schedule with speaker times is on the Schedule tab.
Can I participate remotely?
 Yes. All talks, collaboration, and submissions happen online through Discord and Zoom. The in-person hubs at Collider in New York City and AI Safety Hong Kong are optional.
How do I join the New York City hub?
 Sign up on this page and select the New York City location. Hub details (address, hours, local talks) are shared with registered participants before the sprint.
How do I join the Hong Kong hub?
 Sign up on this page, then register for the venue on the 
AI Safety Hong Kong hub page
.
How do teams work?
 Teams form before or during the sprint. Use the team-forming channels on Discord to find collaborators. Solo is fine. We recommend teams of up to 5, but larger groups are allowed.
Do tracks affect scoring?
 All projects are scored on the same rubric. Tracks orient your project and point you to relevant resources; you compete across all submissions.
What background is required?
 None specific. Every research question is tied to existing papers and open-source code, and the Resources tab has a practical start for each one. See "Who should join" on the Overview tab.
Do I need a GPU or a compute budget?
 No. Every practical start on the Resources tab runs on a normal 16 GB laptop with no GPU, using low-cost API models, with a rough API budget per project.
Are the talks recorded?
 Yes. Recordings are shared on Discord after the event.
What timezone are deadlines in?
 Submissions close Sunday October 25 at 11:59 PM AoE (Anywhere on Earth).
Do I need to attend all three days?
 No. You can work at your own pace. Talks are optional but recommended. The only hard deadline is the Sunday submission cutoff.
Can I participate from any country?
 Yes. The sprint is open globally and runs online.
Submissions
What do I submit?
 A research report in PDF format using the official template. Think of it as a mini research paper documenting your problem, approach, results, and implications, not a product demo. Include the required Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations appendix.
Which template should I use?
 Always use the one linked on the Guidelines tab. The template in any acceptance email may be older.
Will I get a confirmation after submitting?
 Yes. You will get a confirmation with your project title shortly after submitting. If you do not, email 
sprints@apartresearch.com
.
My project doesn't show up on the website after submitting.
 Submissions are published manually and can take up to 12 hours to appear. If it is still missing after that, email 
sprints@apartresearch.com
.
I made a mistake. Can I fix it or update my PDF?
 Yes. Submit again using the exact same title and details, just fix what was wrong. Your new files replace the old ones. If unsure, ask in the help-desk channel and tag @Support first.
Can I add team members after submitting?
 Yes. Update the team list through the submission form. If you need help, ask in the help-desk channel and tag @Support.
Can I submit unfinished work?
 Yes. Submitting something unfinished is always better than not submitting. Judges evaluate what you accomplished in the timeframe; honest limitations are welcome.
Can I build on existing research?
 Yes, but you must clearly identify what is new work done during the sprint. Undisclosed prior work can lead to disqualification.
Can I submit multiple projects?
 Yes, but each needs its own submission with a unique title. Most participants focus on one.
Do you require a particular citation style?
 No. Use whichever style you are comfortable with and stay consistent. Judges care about the substance, not the format.
Judging and results
How does judging work?
 Your project is assigned to expert judges who review your PDF and score it on the rubric above. Judges typically have about a week after the event to complete reviews.
Are individual judge scores shared?
 No. The rubric is public, but individual scores stay internal. Constructive feedback is shared with participants without reviewer names.
When will results be announced?
 Typically 1 to 2 weeks after the judging deadline. Winners are contacted directly, and all participants receive reviewer feedback by email.
Top teams are fast-tracked to the Apart Fellowship. Does that mean we are guaranteed a place?
 Being a top team fast-tracks your project for the fellowship, which is accelerated consideration, not an automatic place. The fellowship runs its own review, and a strong sprint result is a clear positive signal.
