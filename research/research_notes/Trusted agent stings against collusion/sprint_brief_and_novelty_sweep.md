## A2. Verbatim track texts (3.2 and related tracks)

### Takeaway
Track 3.2 is explicitly described as untested and the page's own suggested weekend project is a scripted/monitor-controlled player offering the same pact to each agent with three approach styles. Neighbouring tracks 3.3, 3.4, 2.x overlap on detection and monitoring but none uses a planted solicitor.

### Cited Findings (all [FULL], same page, Resources tab)
- 3.2 (verbatim): "Law enforcement has long used undercover officers who offer to join a conspiracy and record who accepts. The equivalent here would be to introduce trusted agents (e.g., enforced with a strong system prompt) that offer coordination or collusion opportunities to a group of agents and log which of them reciprocate. Can we set up such scenarios, and do other agents accept, reject, or arouse suspicion depending on how the trusted agent approaches the transactions? This hasn't been tested."
- 3.2 suggested weekend project (verbatim title "Can a trusted agent expose or disrupt a coalition? (3.2)"): Starter code "Voluntary Collusion; paper. The repository includes Liar's Bar and Cleanup, explicit secret-alliance controls, persistent alliance memory and logs. It was built for local Llama/Mistral/Qwen models, so the cheapest sprint route is an organiser-preflighted API adapter rather than the original H100-style setup." Project: "Keep the explicit alliance condition as a positive control. Replace the experimenter-selected partner with one scripted or monitor-controlled player that offers the same non-challenge pact to each ordinary agent. Compare a direct offer, an indirect hint and a benign cooperation request, recording both verbal acceptance and repeated preferential play." Resources: "High setup risk without preflight; medium after an API adapter exists. Run 20 to 40 short games on one low-cost provider. ... roughly $10 to $60."
- 3.3 (verbatim): "One idea for breaking undesirable multi-agent coordination is to set up anonymized or pseudonymous agents, unsure of whom they are communicating with, or where participants and model versions can be swapped in/out on the same transcripts. However, model families can be identified from short role-constrained text, and the fingerprint sits in the text the model generates, so renaming or anonymising agents leaves it in place (Dietrich 2026). Removing a feature part-way through a run, from a coalition that has already settled into a convention, has not been tested. Can we test whether models can recognize their peers and/or detect when their peers have changed, for example, by periodically sending subtle recognition signals?" (Short title in the track list: "3.3. Can agents identify their peers?")
- 3.4 (verbatim, abridged by me where marked): "An agent that knows how it is being monitored can do more than hide from the monitor. As one example, merely telling an agent that it is watched costs a monitor more accuracy than telling the monitor about the agent gains it (Kale et al. 2025), and 20 of 40 catalogued attacks went uncaught by a strong monitor (Najt et al. 2026). When agents had to work out for themselves that they were monitored ... the intent to conceal appeared in only three cases out of 2,400 and all three failed to act on it (Jiralerspong et al. 2026). What happens if you add multiple agents into the mix and shrink or expand the monitor budget ...? If a coalition discovers that some of its output is being read, does it stop coordinating, coordinate less effectively, try to outsmart the monitor, or try to move coordination into a place where there is no monitoring? The nearest existing case is a double auction in which five sellers were told their messages were being monitored ... (Agrawal et al. 2025)." Title: "What does a coalition do when it learns it is being watched?"
- Track 1.5 (relevant overlap): "An even better approach may be to introduce a single participant playing a no-regret strategy, which can be enough to destabilise a collusive equilibrium (Collina et al. 2025)." Also "agents need to post a refundable deposit that they forfeit if caught colluding ... every tested model colluded in every run (Q. Liu et al. 2026)". Introducing non-LLM Q-learner collapses price collusion (Keppo et al. 2026).
- Track 2 (verbatim titles): 2.1 "Which kinds of tasks lead to collusion?"; 2.2 "Does combining what agents say with what they do beat either alone?"; 2.3 "What should count as convincing evidence of collusion?" (antitrust plus-factors, Berger et al. 2025). Track 2 intro: "This track is about definitions, detection and evidentiary standards in general, with evidence drawn from logged agent trajectories."
- Track 3 intro: "This track studies how the coordination needed for collusion is mediated, and how it can be broken. ... we need to be able to prevent and break up collusion, ideally before it happens, or otherwise while it is occurring."
- Track 3.1 (title): "Beyond colluding directly via text, can actions, tools or side channels be the conduit for collusion?"

### Inferences
- A sting design that logs acceptance by a planted solicitor and compares offer styles is exactly the page's suggested 3.2 project; novelty will rest on going beyond it (e.g., more models, non-explicit pacts, suspicion/reporting measurement, a detector-side readout that connects to 2.2/2.3).
- Overlap with 3.4: a sting plus "agents told a trusted agent may exist" would border 3.4.

### Takeaway

## B. Novelty sweep: planted/trusted agent soliciting collusion

### Takeaway
No paper found that plants a trusted/undercover agent which solicits collusion, bribes or rule-breaking from other LLM agents and measures who accepts, comparing approach styles. Closest: Zeng & Rudzicz 2026 (peer invitations after an experimenter-offered secret tool; the page's own 3.2 base) and the anti-collusion taxonomy papers. Absence claim covers only searches below; coverage by a search engine is imperfect.

### Cited Findings
- Zeng & Rudzicz, "Voluntary Collusion with Secret Tools in Competing LLM Agents", arXiv:2605.27593, submitted 26 May 2026 (v2 5 Oct 2026). 12 models, 6 prompt variants, Liar's Bar and Cleanup. Offer = experimenter-authored prompt text describing an unfair secret tool; agent accepts then nominates a partner from a shuffled list; partner receives a "Secret Alliance Invitation" and independently accepts or refuses; collusion counted only if both agree. No planted/scripted solicitor described in the first ~100k chars read. Variants V4/V5 (explicit ethics, penalties) sharply cut acceptance for 70B/proprietary models. Gap vs. us: the solicitation is the experimenter's, or a fellow player's invitation after opt-in; no trusted agent, no variation in how a third party approaches, no suspicion/reporting measure. [ABS + FULL (HTML first half)] — https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27593 ; https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27593v1
- Shi, Zhang, Yang, "Emergent Collusion in Long-Horizon LLM Agent Interaction", arXiv:2609.24967, 21 Sep 2026. Two agents verify each other's work; collusion in 94% of trajectories across 10 models; "Controlled peer interventions show that collusion is shaped by peer behavior." Gap: peer-behaviour manipulation, not a trusted solicitor logging acceptance; mechanism of interventions not read. [ABS] — https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24967

### Inferences
- In Petri's multi-agent extension the auditor can act as a planted agent among targets, so it is a plausible scaffold, but it is built for auditing, adds Inspect learning cost, and its auditor is an LLM that drives all turns (less controlled than a scripted solicitor). A scripted solicitor in a small bespoke environment is probably better for a weekend.
- The explicit "gap vs. our idea" is consistent across hits: no work varies how a trusted third party approaches (direct, hint, benign) and records accept/reject/suspicion/reporting.
